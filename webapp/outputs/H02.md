# In-line spectroscopy first assessment

Generated by Claude with RAG over the AFS sources, checked in code. First assessment to support an AFS expert; not a final recommendation.

## Enquiry

Our dairy process handles both a relatively clear whey stream and an opaque milk concentrate. We want a pilot for composition monitoring, using laboratory protein and fat results where those variables are relevant. We can compare different measurement interfaces before choosing the installation. What first measurement concepts and comparison experiment would make sense for these two streams?

## Recommended in-line method

**Test NIR transmission/transflection on the whey stream and NIR diffuse reflection on the milk concentrate, with a separate calibration per stream** [S01 p.17; S01 p.18; S02 p.6; S02 p.7]

| NIR | Whey: NIR transmission/transflection probe with controlled path length (usually 1–10 mm); concentrate: flush-mounted NIR diffuse-reflection probe | In the pipe or vat of each stream, flush-mounted for reflection (exact points unknown – ask) | Vis-NIR low-OH silica (350–2400 nm) |

Test against: A single transflection probe on both streams

## Risks and problems with the request

- **Check first:** The clear whey and the opaque concentrate are two optical regimes: transmission/transflection suits clear liquids and fails on thick or fat-rich product, reflection suits opaque product but is less representative for clear liquids, so plan a separate interface decision and calibration per stream and treat one shared interface as a hypothesis to test. [S01 p.18; S01 p.17]
- **Check first:** NIR reflection on the concentrate is sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size, so scatter correction (MSC/SNV) before PLS and variation of these properties in the calibration set are needed. [S01 p.17]
- **Check first:** NIR gives broad overlapping bands and may lack sensitivity below about 0.1 %, so low protein or fat levels in whey need a chemometric model checked against independent laboratory results. [S01 p.10; S01 p.16; S01 p.5]
- **Check first:** Vis-NIR silica fiber transmits 350–2400 nm while NIR is defined to 2500 nm, so the last part of the NIR range is not covered and the usable window must be checked. [S02 p.6; S01 p.10]

## Methods worth testing

- **NIR transmission/transflection** (Main): Preferred for transparent or semi-transparent dairy liquids such as whey, with controlled 1–10 mm path length. [S01 p.18]
- **NIR diffuse reflection** (Main): Used inline for opaque or concentrated dairy products with a flush-mounted probe, followed by scatter correction and PLS for fat and protein. [S01 p.17]
- **MIR ATR** (Compare): Adds molecular specificity NIR might miss, with protein amide bands near 1640/1548 cm-1 and the ester carbonyl band near 1740 cm-1, but samples only a 1–6 µm layer. [S01 p.18; S04 p.3; S01 Table 1 (p.6)]
- **Raman (e.g. 785 nm excitation; Raman shift range unknown – ask)** (Only if needed): Can add molecular specificity for dairy but signals are weak and may be masked by fluorescence of organic compounds. [S01 p.18; S01 p.11; S01 p.14–15]

## Multispectral option

**NIR + MIR ATR**: Complex media such as milk may need two methods, and MIR ATR adds well-defined molecular bands (protein amide, fat ester carbonyl) to the broad overlapping NIR overtones.. Probe: separate synchronised probes. Fusion: model per method first, then low-level fusion of synchronised spectra with dedicated data-fusion algorithms. Prove the gain: Keep the combination only if the fused model predicts laboratory protein and fat better than the best single NIR model on the same validation samples. [S01 p.7; S01 p.18; S01 p.5; S01 p.8; S01 p.9; S04 p.3; S01 p.15]

## Clarification questions

1. Where exactly would each probe be installed (pipe, vat, line section, diameter) for the whey and the concentrate? (Reflection probes are flush-mounted in pipe or vat and transmission needs a controlled path length, so the location decides the interface.) [S01 p.17; S01 p.18]
2. What protein and fat ranges occur in the whey and in the concentrate, and which variables matter for each stream? (Transmission fails when product is too thick or fat-rich and NIR may lack sensitivity below about 0.1 %.) [S01 p.18; S01 p.16]
3. Which independent laboratory methods produce your protein and fat results, and how many paired samples per stream can you provide? (Quantitative NIR needs chemometric models that are built and validated against reference values.) [S01 p.5]
4. What temperature, pressure, flow and cleaning (CIP) conditions apply at the candidate measurement points? (Probe and fiber choice must withstand the process conditions at the measuring point.) [S02 p.7; S01 p.15]
5. Does fat globule size or concentrate solids content vary between batches? (NIR reflection is sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size, which must be covered in the calibration.) [S01 p.17]

## First feasibility experiment

Compare NIR transmission/transflection and NIR diffuse reflection (with MIR ATR as comparison) on whey and milk concentrate samples against independent laboratory protein and fat results to choose the interface per stream. [S01 p.3; S01 p.18; S01 p.17; S01 p.5; S02 p.20; S05 p.1]

1. Collect paired samples from each stream covering the expected protein and fat range and send them for independent laboratory protein and fat analysis.
2. Measure every sample with an NIR transflection probe and an NIR diffuse-reflection probe on the same NIR spectrometer, and with an MIR ATR probe as comparison.
3. Apply scatter correction (MSC/SNV) where needed and build separate PLS models per stream and per interface.
4. Test whether a single transflection probe gives acceptable models for both streams versus the stream-specific interfaces.
5. Compare single-method models with an NIR + MIR ATR fused model on the same validation data before deciding on an inline pilot.

References: Independent laboratory protein results per sample; Independent laboratory fat results per sample
Go / no-go: Acceptance criteria for prediction error per variable and stream must come from the client.

## Assumptions

- The flowing whey and concentrate are homogeneous enough at the measuring point that time-synchronised separate probes are sufficient, without a combined same-spot probe.
- Laboratory protein and fat results can be paired with spectra from the same samples.
- One NIR spectrometer can serve both transmission and reflection probes.

## Sources cited

- **S01 p.17** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Maintaining both NIR Reflection and NIR Transmission techniques in a lab environment provides a comprehensive analytical suite capable of handling the diverse physical properties of dairy products. - NIR Reflection: Inline NIR Reflection is primarily used for opaque or highly concentrated dairy products, such as yogurt, butter, or whole milk concentrates. The main equipment includes a fiber optic probe where one set of fibers delivers light to the sample and the other collects the "backscattered" light. Experimentally, the probe is flush -mounted into a pipe or vat to interact directly with the flowing medium. Because the light does not penetrate deeply, the method is sensitive to the surface characteristics and fat globule size.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): This technique generally offers higher accuracy for low -concentration components because the light interacts with the entir e volume of the sampled cross-section, providing a more representative chemical signature. - The Advantage of Dual-Technique Availability: having both fiber optic techniques available in a lab rather than relying on one provides the flexibility needed to monitor the entire production chain. While transmission is superior for the high-precision analysis of dilute process streams (like whey filtration), it fails when the milk becomes too "thick" or fat -rich, as the light cannot penetrate the sample. Conversely, reflection can work well in hig hly scattering environments but can be less representative for clear liquids.
- **S01 p.17** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: inline NIR reflection is used for opaque or concentrated products; probe flush-mounted in pipe or vat; sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size; MSC/SNV scatter correction before PLS for fat and protein.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: NIR transmission/transflection is preferred for transparent or semi-transparent liquids such as skim milk, whey or permeate; path length (usually 1–10 mm) must be controlled; it fails when the product is too thick or fat-rich.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: keeping both reflection and transmission lets models be calibrated for each production stage; a transflection probe can in some cases work for both low- and high-fat products; the same NIR spectrometer serves both techniques.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: fluorescence, Raman and MIR ATR can add molecular specificity that NIR might miss.

- **S02 p.20** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): AFS pathway: multi-method lab trials on the customer process, then identification of key spectral features, then a chemometric model, a customised system or IoT sensors.
- **S05 p.1** (S05_AFS_Flyer.pdf): AFS guided pathway: (1) multi-method lab test with the AFS multi-spectral fiber system, (2) selection of key spectral features by multivariate analysis and fusion, (3) tailored solution: Path A AI-driven chemometric model (spectra to real-time composition), Path B customised MSF system (tailored spectrometer set for the customer's own R&D), Path C customised spectral fiber sensors (SFS) for a specific process with remote/cloud control.
- **S01 p.11** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Raman signals are weak and often masked by laser scatter and, for many organic compounds, by much stronger fluorescence; special filters and probe designs are needed. The Raman shift (cm⁻¹) is the spectral axis.
- **S01 p.14–15** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Pharmaceutical example: NIR reflection probe and Raman probe (785 nm excitation) inserted into the feed frame of a rotary tablet press for blend potency; NIR sees hydrogen bonds/polar groups, Raman the molecular backbone/non-polar groups; low-level fusion of synchronised spectra with PLS.
- **S01 p.7** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Complex media (oil, milk, fermentation media, tissue) may need two or more methods; examples: NIR+fluorescence gave more accurate biomass in yeast fermentation and distinguished intra- and extracellular metabolites; NIR+IR showed benefits for tumour boundaries; fluorescence+IR was needed to classify tissues; NIR+Raman gave the best results for pellet coating.
- **S01 p.8** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): For heterogeneous samples the different methods must see the same small area; alternating two probes makes this hard, multispectral probes solve it. Data from methods with different acquisition rates must be synchronised.
- **S01 p.9** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fused data blocks differ in noise and information content; normalisation alone does not fix this, so dedicated data-fusion algorithms are needed.
- **S01 p.15** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): The cited feed-frame study reports that fused NIR+Raman PLS models predicted API potency more accurately than single-method models (in that study).
