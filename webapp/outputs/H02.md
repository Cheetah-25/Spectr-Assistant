# In-line spectroscopy first assessment

Generated by Claude with RAG over the AFS sources, checked in code. First assessment to support an AFS expert; not a final recommendation.

## Enquiry

Our dairy process handles both a relatively clear whey stream and an opaque milk concentrate. We want a pilot for composition monitoring, using laboratory protein and fat results where those variables are relevant. We can compare different measurement interfaces before choosing the installation. What first measurement concepts and comparison experiment would make sense for these two streams?

## Recommended in-line method

**Test NIR transmission/transflection on the whey and NIR diffuse reflection on the concentrate, each with its own calibration** [S01 p.18; S01 p.17; S02 p.6; S02 p.7; S01 p.10]

| Method | Probe / interface | Install | Fiber |
|---|---|---|---|
| NIR | Transmission/transflection probe (path usually 1-10 mm) for whey; flush-mounted diffuse-reflection probe for concentrate | Separate measuring points in the whey line and in the concentrate pipe or vat, chosen after the interface comparison | Vis-NIR silica (350-2400 nm), low-OH silica for long remote cables |

Test against: A single transflection probe used on both streams
Not final yet: Variables, ranges, process conditions, installation points and accuracy needs are unknown, and published dairy NIR use only justifies testing; the method must be proven against independent laboratory analyses on the client's own streams.

Status: Provisional assessment. Materials (whey and opaque concentrate), target (composition, with laboratory protein and fat as reference) and the process are known, so a provisional first assessment is possible; a dairy NIR application is a reason to test, not proof that it works in this process.

Test NIR on both streams with a separate interface per stream: a transmission/transflection probe with controlled path length for the clear whey and a flush-mounted diffuse-reflection probe for the opaque concentrate, both on the same NIR spectrometer. Compare transmission/transflection and reflection on both streams in a lab trial against independent laboratory protein and fat analyses, and build a separate scatter-corrected PLS calibration per stream. Fluorescence, Raman or MIR ATR can be screened alongside as possible additions for molecular specificity. [S01 p.17; S01 p.18]

## Risks and problems with the request

- **Check first:** The clear whey and the opaque concentrate behave differently optically: transmission/transflection suits the clear whey and reflection suits the opaque concentrate, so plan a separate interface decision and a separate calibration per stream, and treat one shared interface as a hypothesis to test. [S01 p.17; S01 p.18]
- **Check first:** Transmission needs a controlled path length and fails if the product becomes too thick or fat-rich, so check whether the concentrate can be measured this way at all. [S01 p.18]
- **Check first:** Reflection sees only a shallow layer and is sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size, so scatter correction (MSC/SNV) and a flush mount in flowing product are needed. [S01 p.17]

## Methods worth testing

- **NIR transmission/transflection** (Main): Preferred for transparent or semi-transparent liquids such as whey, and the volume sampled gives a representative signature for dilute streams. [S01 p.18]
- **NIR diffuse reflection** (Main): Used inline for opaque or concentrated dairy products, with scatter correction before PLS for fat and protein. [S01 p.17]
- **Fluorescence** (Compare): Low-cost and highly sensitive for intrinsic fluorophores of biological origin, and can be combined with NIR to add specificity. [S01 p.18; S01 p.12; S01 Table 1 (p.6)]
- **Raman or MIR ATR** (Only if needed): They add molecular specificity NIR may miss, but Raman can be masked by fluorescence and MIR ATR samples only a few micrometres. [S01 p.18; S01 p.11; S01 p.5]

## Multispectral option

**NIR + Fluorescence**: Milk is a complex medium that may need two methods, and fluorescence can add molecular specificity to NIR's broad bands for major components; in a cited fermentation example, NIR+fluorescence gave more accurate biomass.. Probe: Separate time-synchronised probes for the flowing liquid, unless the concentrate turns out to be heterogeneous at the probe scale, in which case a combined same-spot probe is needed. Fusion: Model per method first, then low-level fusion of synchronised spectra using dedicated data-fusion algorithms. Prove the gain: The fused model must beat the best single NIR model per stream on the same independent validation samples. [S01 p.7; S01 p.18; S01 p.12; S01 p.8; S01 p.9; S01 p.14–15]

## Clarification questions

1. Which composition variables do you need for each stream, and over what ranges? (The target variable and range set the spectral region, the interface and the calibration design.) [S01 p.5; S02 p.18]
2. How much fat and how many solids does the concentrate contain at the planned measuring point? (Transmission fails in thick or fat-rich product, so this decides whether reflection is required.) [S01 p.18]
3. What are the temperature, pressure, flow and pipe or vat geometry at the candidate installation points? (Reflection probes are flush-mounted in pipe or vat, and transflection needs a controlled path length.) [S01 p.17; S01 p.18]
4. Which independent laboratory methods produce your protein and fat results, and can samples be time-matched to the spectra? (Chemometric calibration models must be built and validated against reference values.) [S01 p.5]
5. What accuracy and measurement frequency does the pilot need? (These are the go/no-go criteria for choosing between the interfaces.) [S01 p.3]

## First feasibility experiment

Compare NIR transmission/transflection and NIR diffuse reflection on whey and concentrate samples and decide one interface and one calibration per stream. [S01 p.18; S01 p.17; S01 p.3; S01 p.5; S02 p.20]

1. Collect whey and concentrate samples that cover the expected composition range, and analyse each one independently in the laboratory for protein and fat.
2. Measure each sample with a transflection probe at a controlled path length and with a diffuse-reflection probe, on the same NIR spectrometer.
3. Optionally record fluorescence on the same samples to screen whether it adds information.
4. Apply MSC/SNV scatter correction, build a PLS model per stream and per interface, and validate on independent samples.
5. Compare validated performance per stream against the client's criteria and choose the interface and installation point.

References: Independent laboratory protein analysis of each sample; Independent laboratory fat analysis of each sample
Go / no-go: criteria come from the client

## Sources cited

- **S01 p.17** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: inline NIR reflection is used for opaque or concentrated products; probe flush-mounted in pipe or vat; sensitive to surface characteristics and fat globule size; MSC/SNV scatter correction before PLS for fat and protein.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: NIR transmission/transflection is preferred for transparent or semi-transparent liquids such as skim milk, whey or permeate; path length (usually 1–10 mm) must be controlled; it fails when the product is too thick or fat-rich.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: keeping both reflection and transmission lets models be calibrated for each production stage; a transflection probe can in some cases work for both low- and high-fat products; the same NIR spectrometer serves both techniques.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Dairy: fluorescence, Raman and MIR ATR can add molecular specificity that NIR might miss.
- **S01 p.18** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): This technique generally offers higher accuracy for low -concentration components because the light interacts with the entir e volume of the sampled cross-section, providing a more representative chemical signature. - The Advantage of Dual-Technique Availability: having both fiber optic techniques available in a lab rather than relying on one provides the flexibility needed to monitor the entire production chain. While transmission is superior for the high-precision analysis of dilute process streams (like whey filtration), it fails when the milk becomes too "thick" or fat -rich, as the light cannot penetrate the sample. Conversely, reflection can work well in hig hly scattering environments but can be less representative for clear liquids.
- **S01 p.17** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Maintaining both NIR Reflection and NIR Transmission techniques in a lab environment provides a comprehensive analytical suite capable of handling the diverse physical properties of dairy products. - NIR Reflection: Inline NIR Reflection is primarily used for opaque or highly concentrated dairy products, such as yogurt, butter, or whole milk concentrates. The main equipment includes a fiber optic probe where one set of fibers delivers light to the sample and the other collects the "backscattered" light. Experimentally, the probe is flush -mounted into a pipe or vat to interact directly with the flowing medium. Because the light does not penetrate deeply, the method is sensitive to the surface characteristics and fat globule size.
- **S02 p.6** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): Fiber transmission ranges: UV-Vis silica 190–1200 nm; Vis-NIR silica 350–2400 nm; chalcogenide As-S (CIR) 1.1–6.5 µm; polycrystalline (PIR) 3–17 µm; hollow glass waveguides 2–18 µm; InF₃ 0.3–5.5 µm; ZrF₄ 0.3–4.5 µm (figure values).
- **S02 p.7** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): Robust 100–300 m cables of low-OH silica fibers enable remote industrial process control (Vis-NIR). Al/Cu-coated silica fibers offer high-temperature resistance and no polymer-jacket fluorescence for Raman and fluorescence.
- **S01 p.10** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): NIR (780–2500 nm) is the most common online fiber-probe method; its broad overlapping overtone/combination bands require chemometrics; diffuse reflectance allows measurements on scattering samples.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Selecting a spectral method for a specific analytical problem requires considering many factors and specialised knowledge.
- **S02 p.18** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): Band chart: fat/protein/moisture 0.85–1.1 µm; moisture/polymers/API/ethanol 1.3–1.7, 1.5–2.0, 1.7–2.2 µm; hydrocarbons 3.0–3.7 µm; ethanol/glucose/organic acids 6.0–8.0, 8.0–10.5 µm. A central analyzer with long fibers to many points is contrasted with distributed IoT sensors.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Mid-IR spectra contain well-defined absorption peaks, while NIR, UV and fluorescence give broad overlapping spectra; quantitative use requires chemometric calibration or classification models that are built, validated and then applied.
- **S01 p.3** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): For samples of incompletely known composition it is not always possible to predict which method will work; testing several optical approaches is the way to select the most suitable method or combination.
- **S02 p.20** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): AFS pathway: multi-method lab trials on the customer process, then identification of key spectral features, then a chemometric model, a customised system or IoT sensors.
- **S05 p.1** (S05_AFS_Flyer.pdf): AFS guided pathway: (1) multi-method lab test with the AFS multi-spectral fiber system, (2) selection of key spectral features by multivariate analysis and fusion, (3) tailored solution: Path A AI-driven chemometric model (spectra to real-time composition), Path B customised MSF system (tailored spectrometer set for the customer's own R&D), Path C customised spectral fiber sensors (SFS) for a specific process with remote/cloud control.
- **S01 p.12** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fluorimetry measures broad emission from certain samples under UV/visible excitation; it is frequently used in biology and biotechnology and can be combined with MIR or NIR.
- **S01 Table 1 (p.6)** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Table 1 rates methods qualitatively: MIR ATR (liquid/paste/soft, depth 1–6 µm, sensitivity average, selectivity high, cost high); NIR transmission/transflection probe (liquid/colloidal, 1–10 mm); NIR diffuse reflection probe (scattering media/powder, 0.5–1 mm); Raman probe (liquid/powder/solid, 2–5 mm, selectivity high, cost high); UV/Vis probes (selectivity low); fluorimetric probe (liquid/solid, 1–5 mm, sensitivity high, selectivity low, cost low).
- **S01 p.11** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Raman signals are weak and often masked by laser scatter and, for many organic compounds, by much stronger fluorescence; special filters and probe designs are needed. The Raman shift (cm⁻¹) is the spectral axis.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Strong mid-IR absorption limits MIR to very thin layers (usually <50 µm), which is why MIR is used with ATR attachments and probes.
- **S01 p.7** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Complex media (oil, milk, fermentation media, tissue) may need two or more methods; examples: NIR+fluorescence gave more accurate biomass in yeast fermentation and distinguished intra- and extracellular metabolites; NIR+IR showed benefits for tumour boundaries; fluorescence+IR was needed to classify tissues; NIR+Raman gave the best results for pellet coating.
- **S01 p.8** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): For heterogeneous samples the different methods must see the same small area; alternating two probes makes this hard, multispectral probes solve it. Data from methods with different acquisition rates must be synchronised.
- **S01 p.9** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fused data blocks differ in noise and information content; normalisation alone does not fix this, so dedicated data-fusion algorithms are needed.
- **S01 p.14–15** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Pharmaceutical example: NIR reflection probe and Raman probe (785 nm excitation) inserted into the feed frame of a rotary tablet press for blend potency; NIR sees hydrogen bonds/polar groups, Raman the molecular backbone/non-polar groups; low-level fusion of synchronised spectra with PLS.
