## Risks and problems with the request

- **Won't work:** The kidney study is not proof of surgical readiness: it tested two prototypes on 14 in vitro samples from 7 patients, measured thawed frozen biopsies whose spectra can differ from in vivo, and its authors call it a proof of concept with very high variance in cancer tissue, unacceptable false negatives and much work remaining. [S03 p.1; S03 p.3; S03 p.14]
- **Wrong assumption:** The study can only be quoted as an in vitro proof of concept that an LED-based NIR sensor captured tumour-related variance in kidney samples, the S01 surgical scenario is only an application concept, and neither applies to a different sensor, organ or set-up without new data. [S03 p.1; S01 p.13; S03 p.14]
- **Check first:** The LED wavelengths of the proposed sensor must be re-selected for the client's tissue and cancer type from full-spectrum laboratory data, because the optimal LED set differs per case. [S03 p.14; S01 p.9; S03 p.10]
- **Check first:** With limited samples, models need sample-based segmented cross-validation and randomisation tests against independent histology before any performance can be claimed. [S03 p.14; S03 p.9]

## Methods worth testing

- **NIR diffuse reflectance (LED / full spectrum)** (Main): It is the method of the kidney study, penetrates tissue up to about 3 mm and works with remote silica fiber probes. [S03 p.2; S01 p.5; S01 p.10]
- **MIR ATR** (Compare): Its well-defined absorption bands of thin soft-tissue layers (e.g. amide and ester carbonyl bands) add specificity, and NIR+IR showed benefits for tumour boundaries. [S01 p.5; S04 p.3; S01 p.7]
- **Fluorescence** (Compare): It is highly sensitive to intrinsic biological fluorophores such as FAD and porphyrins and can be combined with MIR or NIR. [S01 p.5; S04 p.3; S01 p.12]
- **Raman** (Only if needed): It has high selectivity but weak signals often masked by fluorescence in organic samples, so it needs special probes and filters. [S01 Table 1 (p.6); S01 p.11]

## Multispectral option

**MIR ATR + fluorescence (with NIR as the single-method baseline)**: IR bands give molecular structure (fat ester carbonyl, protein amides) while fluorescence sees biological fluorophores, and only the fused data separated all three tissue classes in the combi-probe study.. Probe: combined probe (same spot). Fusion: low-level fusion of synchronised spectra. Prove the gain: The fused model must give fewer false negatives than the best single method (NIR) on the same patient-based cross-validation data, using dedicated fusion algorithms rather than normalisation alone. [S04 p.4; S04 p.2; S01 p.8; S01 p.9; S01 p.7; S02 p.15]

## Clarification questions

1. Which organ and cancer type will the sensor be used on, and is it the same kidney tumour type as in the study? (The LED set must be adapted to each tissue and cancer type and results do not transfer to another organ.) [S03 p.14]
2. What are the LED wavelengths, probe geometry and spectrometer of your proposed sensor? (The study prototypes cannot represent a different sensor design without new data.) [S03 p.1; S03 p.14]
3. How many paired tumour and normal tissue samples with histological diagnosis can you provide, from how many patients? (Histology is the independent reference, and validation with limited data needs patient-based segmented cross-validation.) [S03 p.9; S03 p.14]
4. Can samples be measured fresh rather than frozen and thawed? (Spectra of frozen/thawed biopsies can differ from real in vivo measurements.) [S03 p.3]
5. What false-negative rate and specificity would your clinical team accept for a boundary decision? (False negatives are unacceptable in oncology, so go/no-go criteria must come from the client.) [S03 p.14]

## First feasibility experiment

Establish in the laboratory whether the client's NIR sensor concept discriminates tumour from normal tissue of the target organ, before any in vivo work. [S03 p.10; S03 p.9; S03 p.14; S03 p.3; S01 p.9; S05 p.1]

1. Collect paired tumour and normal ex vivo samples from several patients with histology as the independent reference, preferably measured fresh.
2. Measure each sample at several points with a full-spectrum NIR diffuse-reflectance fiber probe and, for comparison, a combined MIR ATR + fluorescence probe.
3. Select the informative wavelengths and simulate the LED sensor channels from the full spectra using the real LED emission spectra as weights.
4. Build PCA and PLS-DA models and validate them with patient-based segmented cross-validation and randomisation tests.
5. Compare false negatives and variance in cancer tissue against the client's go/no-go criteria before planning any in vivo study.

References: Histopathological diagnosis of each sample (independent laboratory analysis)
Go / no-go: criteria come from the client

- **S01 p.7** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Complex media (oil, milk, fermentation media, tissue) may need two or more methods; examples: NIR+fluorescence gave more accurate biomass in yeast fermentation and distinguished intra- and extracellular metabolites; NIR+IR showed benefits for tumour boundaries; fluorescence+IR was needed to classify tissues; NIR+Raman gave the best results for pellet coating.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fluorimetry is highly sensitive but relies on secondary emission of certain substances, mostly of biological origin; most chemicals do not fluoresce.
- **S01 p.12** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fluorimetry measures broad emission from certain samples under UV/visible excitation; it is frequently used in biology and biotechnology and can be combined with MIR or NIR.
- **S01 Table 1 (p.6)** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Table 1 rates methods qualitatively: MIR ATR (liquid/paste/soft, depth 1–6 µm, sensitivity average, selectivity high, cost high); NIR transmission/transflection probe (liquid/colloidal, 1–10 mm); NIR diffuse reflection probe (scattering media/powder, 0.5–1 mm); Raman probe (liquid/powder/solid, 2–5 mm, selectivity high, cost high); UV/Vis probes (selectivity low); fluorimetric probe (liquid/solid, 1–5 mm, sensitivity high, selectivity low, cost low).
- **S01 p.11** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Raman signals are weak and often masked by laser scatter and, for many organic compounds, by much stronger fluorescence; special filters and probe designs are needed. The Raman shift (cm⁻¹) is the spectral axis.
