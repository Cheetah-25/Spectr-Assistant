Our only justification is that fluorescence can be very sensitive. We have not identified a fluorescent target, marker or reference relationship. What should the assistant tell us?

## Recommended in-line method

**Test Raman for glucose in the fermentation broth, compared with NIR, before any in-line installation.** [S01 p.16; S01 p.11; S01 p.5; S02 p.6; S02 p.7; S01 Table 1 (p.6); S01 p.3]

| Method | Probe / interface | Install | Fiber |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raman | Raman fiber probe with filters designed to suppress laser scatter and fluorescence | Initially on laboratory fermentation samples, later in a bioreactor port once the client defines it | Vis-NIR silica (350-2400 nm), preferably Al/Cu-coated to avoid polymer-jacket fluorescence; excitation wavelength and Raman shift range to be defined in the test |

Test against: NIR transmission/transflection probe

Sensitivity alone does not justify fluorescence: it is rated low in selectivity, works only for fluorescent components, and in the supplied bioprocess material glucose is assigned to NIR and Raman while fluorescence targets NADH, tryptophan and riboflavin. Test Raman (with NIR as comparison) for glucose first, and treat your fluorescence spectrometer at most as a supporting signal for biomass combined with NIR, validated against an independent viable-cell reference. [S01 Table 1 (p.6); S01 p.5; S04 p.4; S01 p.16; S01 p.7]

## Risks and problems with the request

- **Won't work:** High sensitivity is not selectivity: fluorimetry is rated high in sensitivity but low in selectivity and only works when the sample has a fluorescent component, which you have not identified. [S01 Table 1 (p.6); S01 p.5; S04 p.4]
- **Won't work:** There is no direct fluorescence route to glucose in the sources: fluorescence targets NADH, tryptophan and riboflavin, while glucose is assigned to NIR and Raman. [S01 p.16]
- **Check first:** Viable biomass needs a defined signal and reference: the sources link biomass to turbidity (cell density) and report better biomass estimates from NIR plus fluorescence, but describe no viable-cell measurement, so an independent viability reference is required. [S01 p.16; S01 p.7]
- **Check first:** Fluorescence, NIR and UV give broad overlapping spectra, so any quantitative result needs a chemometric model built and validated against reference analyses. [S01 p.5]

## Methods worth testing

- **Raman** (Main): Raman gives direct detection of glucose, acetate or lactate and suits aqueous media because water absorbs weakly. [S01 p.16]
- **NIR** (Compare): NIR tracks major components such as glucose in the millimolar range and is the most common online fiber-probe method, but may lack sensitivity below 0.1 %. [S01 p.16; S01 p.10]
- **UV-Vis** (Only if needed): UV/Vis turbidity correlates with cell density and can be a low-cost biomass indicator, with probe choice depending on optical density. [S01 p.16]
- **Fluorescence** (Only if needed): Fluorescence is suitable for intrinsic fluorophores such as NADH, tryptophan and riboflavin and may support biomass only in combination with NIR, not as a stand-alone glucose or viability measurement. [S01 p.16; S01 p.7; S01 p.12]

## Multispectral option

**NIR + Fluorescence**: NIR tracks major components while fluorescence detects intrinsic fluorophores reflecting the metabolic state, and this combination gave more accurate biomass in yeast fermentation.. Probe: separate synchronised probes, unless the broth proves heterogeneous, in which case a combined multispectral probe is needed to see the same spot. Fusion: low-level fusion of synchronised spectra with a dedicated data-fusion algorithm, compared with a model per method. Prove the gain: The fused model must predict the biomass reference better than the best single-method model on the same independent validation samples. [S01 p.7; S01 p.16; S01 p.8; S01 p.9; S01 p.15]

## Clarification questions

1. How do you measure viable biomass in the laboratory today, and can you provide those reference values for fermentation samples? (The sources describe no viable-cell measurement, so a model needs an independent viability reference.) [S01 p.16; S01 p.5]
2. Which organism and medium do you use, and what glucose concentration range must be reported with what accuracy? (NIR tracks glucose in the millimolar range but may lack sensitivity below 0.1 %, which decides between NIR and Raman.) [S01 p.16]
3. Which excitation wavelength and spectral range does your fluorescence spectrometer cover? (Fluorescence only helps if an intrinsic fluorophore such as NADH, tryptophan or riboflavin is excited and detected.) [S01 p.16; S01 p.12]
4. Which bioreactor port, temperature and sterilisation conditions must an in-line probe withstand? (Each method needs its own probe interface and the task decides the technique.) [S01 p.4]
5. How turbid is the broth at the end of fermentation? (Optical density decides between transmission/transflection and other probe types.) [S01 p.16; S01 Table 1 (p.6)]

## First feasibility experiment

Find out whether Raman or NIR sees glucose changes in your fermentation broth and whether NIR plus fluorescence tracks biomass against an independent reference. [S02 p.20; S05 p.1; S01 p.5; S01 p.3]

1. Collect fermentation samples across the run, covering the expected glucose and biomass ranges.
2. Measure each sample with Raman and NIR fiber probes and record fluorescence with your existing spectrometer at the same time.
3. Analyse every sample independently in the laboratory for glucose and for viable biomass with your reference method.
4. Build chemometric models per method and for the NIR plus fluorescence combination, and validate them on samples not used for calibration.

References: Independent laboratory glucose analysis; Independent laboratory viable-biomass analysis defined by the client
Go / no-go: criteria come from the client

## Sources cited

- **S01 Table 1 (p.6)** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Table 1 rates methods qualitatively: MIR ATR (liquid/paste/soft, depth 1–6 µm, sensitivity average, selectivity high, cost high); NIR transmission/transflection probe (liquid/colloidal, 1–10 mm); NIR diffuse reflection probe (scattering media/powder, 0.5–1 mm); Raman probe (liquid/powder/solid, 2–5 mm, selectivity high, cost high); UV/Vis probes (selectivity low); fluorimetric probe (liquid/solid, 1–5 mm, sensitivity high, selectivity low, cost low).
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fluorimetry is highly sensitive but relies on secondary emission of certain substances, mostly of biological origin; most chemicals do not fluoresce.
- **S04 p.4** (S04_Combi_MIR_Fluorescence_2021.pdf): Neither method alone separated all three tissue classes in PCA; the fused data did. The approach is limited to samples with at least one fluorescent component and to samples suitable for ATR (liquids, soft solids).
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Bioprocess: fluorescence detects intrinsic fluorophores such as NADH, tryptophan and riboflavin at high sensitivity (nM–µM).
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Bioprocess: Raman gives direct detection of metabolites such as glucose, acetate or lactate and suits aqueous media because water absorbs weakly.
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Bioprocess: NIR tracks major components such as glucose or glycerol in the millimolar range but may lack sensitivity below 0.1 %.
- **S01 p.7** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Complex media (oil, milk, fermentation media, tissue) may need two or more methods; examples: NIR+fluorescence gave more accurate biomass in yeast fermentation and distinguished intra- and extracellular metabolites; NIR+IR showed benefits for tumour boundaries; fluorescence+IR was needed to classify tissues; NIR+Raman gave the best results for pellet coating.
- **S01 p.11** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Raman signals are weak and often masked by laser scatter and, for many organic compounds, by much stronger fluorescence; special filters and probe designs are needed. The Raman shift (cm⁻¹) is the spectral axis.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Raman needs special probes, is recorded in the visible/NIR region, and carries information complementary to MIR.
- **S02 p.6** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): Fiber transmission ranges: UV-Vis silica 190–1200 nm; Vis-NIR silica 350–2400 nm; chalcogenide As-S (CIR) 1.1–6.5 µm; polycrystalline (PIR) 3–17 µm; hollow glass waveguides 2–18 µm; InF₃ 0.3–5.5 µm; ZrF₄ 0.3–4.5 µm (figure values).
- **S02 p.7** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): Robust 100–300 m cables of low-OH silica fibers enable remote industrial process control (Vis-NIR). Al/Cu-coated silica fibers offer high-temperature resistance and no polymer-jacket fluorescence for Raman and fluorescence.
- **S01 p.3** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): For samples of incompletely known composition it is not always possible to predict which method will work; testing several optical approaches is the way to select the most suitable method or combination.
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Bioprocess: UV/Vis can be a low-cost tool for real-time biomass monitoring because cell-suspension turbidity correlates with cell density.
- **S01 p.5** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Mid-IR spectra contain well-defined absorption peaks, while NIR, UV and fluorescence give broad overlapping spectra; quantitative use requires chemometric calibration or classification models that are built, validated and then applied.
- **S01 p.12** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fluorimetry measures broad emission from certain samples under UV/visible excitation; it is frequently used in biology and biotechnology and can be combined with MIR or NIR.
- **S01 p.4** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): NIR, MIR and Raman differ in absorption and penetration depth, so each needs its own interface (ATR, transmission, transflection, reflection probes); the task decides the method and technique.
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): UV-Vis probe choice follows optical density: transmission/transflection (1–10 mm path) for transparent liquids; UV-ATR for opaque, turbid or concentrated media where transmission saturates.
- **S02 p.20** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): AFS pathway: multi-method lab trials on the customer process, then identification of key spectral features, then a chemometric model, a customised system or IoT sensors.
- **S05 p.1** (S05_AFS_Flyer.pdf): AFS guided pathway: (1) multi-method lab test with the AFS multi-spectral fiber system, (2) selection of key spectral features by multivariate analysis and fusion, (3) tailored solution: Path A AI-driven chemometric model (spectra to real-time composition), Path B customised MSF system (tailored spectrometer set for the customer's own R&D), Path C customised spectral fiber sensors (SFS) for a specific process with remote/cloud control.
- **S02 p.3** (S02_AFS_Process_Control_Slides.pdf): AFS multispectral fiber systems test four methods (Raman, FTIR, fluorescence, NIR) in 0.3–16 µm on the customer process; comparing the spectral data is used to select the process-control solution.
- **S01 p.7** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): This often provides a synergistic effect, allowing one to solve a problem that either method alone cannot handle or only partially. Thus, the combination of NIR spectroscopy with fluorescence spectroscopy demonstrated the possibility of more accurately determining the biomass content during yeast fermentation, as well as distinguishing between their intra- and extracellular metabolites. The combination of NIR and IR spectroscopy showed benefits in determining tumor boundaries in medical diagnostics. The combination of fluorescence and IR spectroscopy also proved necessary for classifying biological tissues.
- **S01 p.10** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): NIR (780–2500 nm) is the most common online fiber-probe method; its broad overlapping overtone/combination bands require chemometrics; diffuse reflectance allows measurements on scattering samples.
- **S01 p.16** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): - Precision Monitoring Across Concentration Ranges: labs equipped with both Near-Infrared (NIR) and Fluorescence spectroscopy can solve the problem of monitoring analytes at widely varying concentrations. NIR spectroscopy is effective for tracking major components like glucose or glycerol in the millimola r range but may lack the sensitivity to detect trace compounds below 0.1 percent. To fill this gap, Fluorescence spectroscopy can be used for high- sensitivity detection (down to the nM -µM range) of critical c ofactors and amino acids like NADH, tryptophan, and riboflavin. By using both, a researcher can maintain high -precision control over both the main carbon source and the delicate metabolic state of the cells.
- **S01 p.8** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): For heterogeneous samples the different methods must see the same small area; alternating two probes makes this hard, multispectral probes solve it. Data from methods with different acquisition rates must be synchronised.
- **S01 p.9** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): Fused data blocks differ in noise and information content; normalisation alone does not fix this, so dedicated data-fusion algorithms are needed.
- **S01 p.15** (S01_Multispectral_Laboratory.pdf): The cited feed-frame study reports that fused NIR+Raman PLS models predicted API potency more accurately than single-method models (in that study).
